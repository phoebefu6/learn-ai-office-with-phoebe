Part 1 · covers where the time actually is

Most of your week is not reachable, and that is the first surprise

6 min live

Every promise about time saved quietly assumes your week is full of the kind of work this tool is good at. It is not. Before we talk about speed, we have to be honest about the size of the surface.

LiveWhy the reachable slice is smaller than it feels4 min▶

The tool is genuinely good at a narrow band of things: turning your material into prose, turning prose into structure, summarizing something you already have, and drafting a first pass you will edit. That band is real and useful.

What it is not, and this is where the week disappears:

  • Deciding. The half hour you spend working out what the report should even say is not draftable, because nobody has told the tool what you know.
  • Waiting. For the finance number, for the approval, for the person who owns the exception. No amount of drafting speed moves this.
  • Being in the room. Which brings us to the claim you have almost certainly been told, and which is measurably false.
Real world

Microsoft ran a randomized trial across 56 firms and over 6,000 workers for six months. Email reading fell by 12.3 minutes a week. Total meeting time moved by plus 2.38 minutes a week and was not statistically significant. In a separate telemetry study of 47 organizations, 10 saw meetings fall and 14 saw meetings rise.

Nobody is going to give you your meetings back. Anyone selling you that is selling.

Part 2 · covers the ask itself

The ask that earns the time, and the ask that wastes it

6 min live

Inside that narrow reachable band, the difference between people who get real value and people who conclude it is overhyped is almost entirely one thing: whether the ask carried any context.

"Summarise this month's numbers for the weekly report."

Back comes a confident page of prose. Two of the figures are not in your data at all, the tone is a press release, and the one exception your boss will ask about is missing entirely.

"Here is last month's sheet. Summarise for the ops lead in five bullets. Keep every figure exactly as it appears. Flag anything that moved more than 10 percent. If a number is missing, say so and do not estimate."

Five bullets, figures intact, two flags, and one honest gap you can go and fill.

LiveThe four things a working ask always carries4 min▶
  • The material. Attach it, paste it, or point at it. On the free Copilot Chat tier this matters twice over, because Microsoft states it "is not grounded in organizational content like files, emails, or chats as part of the chat experience - it is grounded in data from the web only". You have to bring the data to it.
  • The reader. "For the ops lead" and "for the board" produce genuinely different documents. Unnamed means written for nobody.
  • The shape. Five bullets. One page. A table with these three columns. Shape is the cheapest instruction there is and it removes most of the rewriting.
  • The honesty clause. "If a number is missing, say so and do not estimate." This one sentence is the difference between a gap you can see and a gap that has been smoothly filled in for you.

The honesty clause matters more than it sounds, and session 2 is largely about why: the documented failure mode of these tools on long material is omission rather than fabrication. Things quietly go missing, and missing things do not look wrong.

A four-word ask is not efficient, it is under-specified. The version in the right-hand pane above is forty-two words and it is the one that saves you the afternoon.

Build-along

Watch the gap open, then close it on your own report

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The simulator below runs the same levers in two modes. One task is what a lab experiment measures. Your whole week is what actually shows up in your life. Start in task mode with everything off, switch every lever on, and then flip to week mode without changing anything else.

170 minutes, 8 percent faster. You opened it and typed a bare ask. This is most people's entire experience of the technology, and it is why most people are unimpressed.

150 minutes, 19 percent. Attach the data, name the reader. One change, eleven points, and it is the biggest single lever in this course.

137 minutes, 26 percent. Ask section by section instead of for the whole document, because the middle of a long file gets less attention. Session 2.

128 minutes, 31 percent. Make it show the formula or the source line. You cannot defend a number whose derivation you never saw. Session 3.

122 minutes, 34 percent. Check the figures before it goes out. Costs minutes, saves more. This is the ceiling until session 6.

111 minutes, 40 percent. A saved prompt you reuse instead of rewriting. Now you are at the top of the published lab band, on this one task.

Get to 40 percent in task mode. Switch every lever on. Note how good that number looks, and note that it is a real number, honestly modelled on published single-task experiments.

Now flip to week mode and change nothing else. The dial defaults to two days a week, which is roughly how often people actually reach for it. Watch 40 percent become 2.3 percent of your working hours.

Drag the days dial from 1 to 5. One day a week is 27 minutes. Five days is 136. Nothing about the tool changed across that drag. Only how often you reached for it.

Switch on "expect it to give you fewer meetings". The number will not move, and the simulator will tell you exactly which study says so. This lever exists to be switched on and disappoint you.

Now do it for real. Open your actual weekly report. Time yourself honestly on the last one you produced, or estimate to the nearest quarter hour. That is your baseline and you only get one.

Rewrite the ask with all four parts. Material, reader, shape, honesty clause. Send it once. Do not iterate yet, that is session 2.

Count what you actually had to fix. Wrong figures, missing exceptions, wrong tone. This number is the one that tells you whether you saved anything, and it is more useful than any vendor statistic.

If you take one thing from tonight. Attach the data and name the reader. It is eleven points of the forty, it takes ten extra seconds, and almost nobody does it.

Homework

Before session 2

20 min
  1. Pick the report. The real weekly one. Write down what it is, who reads it, and how long it honestly takes you. Session 2 starts from that.
  2. Find out which tier you are actually on. In Microsoft 365 the label reads Copilot (Premium), Copilot (Basic), or Copilot Chat (Basic), and they are very different products. On Google, check whether your edition includes Gemini in Sheets, because Business Starter does not.
  3. Run the four-part ask once. Material, reader, shape, honesty clause. Keep both the ask and what came back.
  4. Count your fixes. How many figures were wrong, and how many things you knew mattered were simply absent. Keep the second number. Session 2 is about that one.
  5. Count your days. Over the next week, tick each day you actually reached for it. That tick count is the honest version of every productivity claim you have ever been shown.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A study says 40 percent faster and your week feels unchanged. What is the most likely explanation?

Both figures can be honest at once. Single-task experiments genuinely find 25 to 40 percent. Measured workweeks find a few percent, because the reachable slice is small and usage is intermittent. The gap is dilution, not deception.

2 · What did Microsoft's own randomized trial find about meeting time?

56 firms, over 6,000 workers, six months. Email reading fell 12.3 minutes a week. Meetings did not move. A separate telemetry study of 47 organizations found 10 where meetings fell and 14 where they rose.

3 · Which single change to your ask is worth the most?

Eleven points of the forty in the simulator, and about ten seconds of effort. Without the material it invents plausible numbers, and without a named reader it writes for nobody, which is what you then spend the afternoon rewriting.
