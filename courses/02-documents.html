The paragraph that went missing

Everyone worries about the made-up number, because a made-up number is loud. It argues with your data and you catch it. Tonight is about the quiet failure instead: the exception in paragraph nine that simply is not in the summary, and never announced itself on the way out. A missing thing does not look wrong. It looks finished. By the end of the 45 minutes your report's written half is drafted, and you have a repeatable way to prove nothing important fell out of it.

Where we left the report

LiveThe one sentence this session hangs on2 min▶

Here it is, and everything else tonight is a consequence of it:

The real failure mode is omission, not invention. A thing that is missing never looks wrong.

Read a summary that contains a wrong figure and something in you snags on it. The number disagrees with the sheet open in the other window, or with what you remember from the meeting, and you go and check. That friction is the safety mechanism, and it works.

Now read a summary with a paragraph quietly absent. Nothing snags. Every sentence on the page is true. The tone is right, the structure is right, the figures that are there are correct. You send it. Three days later somebody in a meeting asks about the exception, and you find out you have been confidently summarising nine tenths of a document.

Your instincts are calibrated for the loud failure. This session recalibrates them for the quiet one.

Part 1 · covers what happens to a long file

What actually happens when you hand over forty pages

6 min live

This is not a mystery and it is not a rumour. Microsoft publishes the mechanism on its own support site, in a page whose entire purpose is to tell you to keep documents short.

LiveMicrosoft's own words, and why they matter more than a blog post4 min▶

From Microsoft's guidance on the length of documents you give to Copilot, three things worth reading slowly.

  • Large language models "tend to prioritize content that is at the beginning and end of a file". That is the mechanism, stated by the vendor, not inferred by a critic.
  • Because of that, "the results you get may give less attention to content that was in the middle of a long file". Less attention, not no attention. It degrades rather than fails.
  • And the blunt version: sometimes Copilot "will focus only on the beginning of the document and then ignore anything beyond that".

Sit with the third one. Not a garbled answer. Not an error message. A clean, well-written summary of the first few pages, presented with exactly the same confidence it would use for the whole thing.

Notice what the vendor is quietly telling you to do: make the file shorter, or make the ask smaller. You cannot make your source document shorter. You can absolutely make the ask smaller, and that is the entire technique in Part 3.

Self-studyLearn the shape of the risk, not a rule of thumb3 min▶

People want a number here. How many pages is too many. There is a very widely repeated word count for Word documents floating around the internet and it does not appear on any live Microsoft page, so this course will not teach it to you. A confident fake threshold is worse than no threshold, because it tells you exactly when to stop worrying.

Learn the shape instead, and the shape is stable:

  • Risk rises with length. A two-page memo is fine. A forty-page contract is not the same activity.
  • Risk concentrates in the middle. Openings and conclusions are the safest part of any long file.
  • Risk is highest for the thing that appears once. Repeated themes survive because they show up at the top and the bottom too. The single-sentence carve-out, the one region that missed target, the one client who churned: these appear exactly once, in the middle, and they are usually the reason anyone reads your report.

That last point is the cruel part. The content most likely to be dropped is the content most worth keeping.

Part 2 · covers the evidence

Somebody measured this, carefully, and it is not comfortable reading

5 min live

Vendor guidance tells you the mechanism. An independent study tells you the size. The Gavel study put twelve frontier models against one hundred long cases and paid for one hundred and sixty hours of human annotation to grade the results properly.

LiveThe finding, stated exactly, then the honest caveat4 min▶

The two lines from the study that matter:

  • "current models are more prone to omitting key information than hallucinating"
  • "The main failure mode is omission rather than hallucination"

Two more findings from the same work. Performance declines as length increases, which lines up exactly with what Microsoft says about long files. And the best model reached an F1 of 59.1 even with a checklist in the prompt, meaning the models were handed a list of what to look for and still did not come back with all of it.

That last detail is worth a moment. The checklist helped. It did not solve it. Which is why Part 3 has an audit step after the checklist step, rather than stopping at the checklist and calling the job done.

Real world

Say this out loud when you teach it. The Gavel study measured legal summarization. It did not measure weekly reports, board packs or project updates. The claim that this transfers to your office documents is an argument this course makes, not a finding the paper reports. The argument is that the mechanism is the same one Microsoft describes for any long file, and that a buried carve-out in a contract and a buried exception in an operations report are the same shape of content. That is reasoning, and you are allowed to disagree with it.

Here is why the caveat is not a weakness. If you overstate this, somebody in your team checks the paper, finds it is about case law, and stops believing the rest of what you told them. Stating the limit is how the useful part survives contact with a sceptic.

Self-studyWhy omission is the harder problem to fix3 min▶

Detecting invention is a comparison problem. You have the output and you have the source, and you check whether each claim in the output appears in the source. Tedious, but bounded, and you can do it with the two documents side by side.

Detecting omission is a recall problem, and recall problems are open-ended. You are not checking the page in front of you. You are trying to notice the absence of something you were not thinking about. There is no highlighted word to look at. The only reliable way to catch it is to decide what must be there before you read the summary, which is why the audit in Part 3 starts with you writing a list from memory.

This is also why "read it carefully" is not a method. Careful reading catches contradictions. It does not catch silence.

Part 3 · covers the fix

The fix is mechanical, and it is four moves

4 min live

You do not need better prose or a cleverer prompt. You need to stop asking one enormous question and start asking several small answerable ones, then check the answers against a list you wrote yourself.

LiveSlice, Ask, Audit, Fill4 min▶
  • Slice. Name the section. "Summarise the operations section only, pages 12 to 18." Every slice you ask for has its own beginning and its own end, and beginnings and ends are the part that gets prioritized. You are not making it smarter, you are moving your content out of the bad neighbourhood.
  • Ask with a checklist. Tell it what must appear. "This summary must cover: the headcount change, the two regions that missed target, the pricing carve-out, the open risk on the vendor contract." The Gavel study shows a checklist helps and does not close the gap, so this is step two of four, not the whole method.
  • Audit. Ask it directly: "What did you leave out of this section, and why?" Then check your own list, item by item, against what came back. Your list first, its answer second, in that order, because a list written after you read the summary is contaminated by the summary.
  • Fill. Whatever is missing, you paste in yourself, in your own words. This is not a failure of the method. This is the method working: the gap became visible, which is the only thing you ever wanted.

The honesty clause from session 1, translated for documents. "If a number is missing, say so and do not estimate" becomes "if a section is thin or you are unsure you covered it, say so rather than smoothing over it". Same instruction, different failure mode. Carry both.

The ask that loses things Summarise this document for my weekly report.
The ask that survives an audit Summarise the operations section only, pages 12 to 18 of the attached file. Five bullets, for the ops lead. This summary must cover, or explicitly say it is absent: 1. the headcount change 2. any region that missed target 3. the pricing carve-out 4. the open risk on the vendor contract 5. anything that changed by more than 10 percent Keep every figure exactly as it appears. If a section is thin or you are unsure you covered it, say so rather than smoothing over it. Then, separately: what did you leave out of this section, and why?

Ninety seconds of extra typing. Seven points on the ladder. And the fourth line of that checklist is the one that saves you a meeting in three weeks' time.

"Summarise this 38-page quarterly review for my weekly report."

Back comes a fluent page. The opening context is beautifully captured, the closing recommendations are there, and the tone is exactly right. Nothing in it is false. The vendor risk in section 4 and the one region that missed target are simply not mentioned, and nothing on the page hints that they ever existed.

"Summarise the operations section only, pages 12 to 18, in five bullets for the ops lead. It must cover headcount, any region that missed target, the pricing carve-out and the vendor risk, or say each one is absent. Then tell me what you left out and why."

Five bullets. Four of your five items covered with figures intact. The fifth comes back as "the pricing carve-out is not discussed in this section", which is true, and tells you exactly which other pages you now have to ask about.

Build-along

Draft the written half, then prove nothing fell out of it

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open the real document. The long one that feeds your weekly report: the project log, the quarterly pack, the meeting notes nobody reads, the system export. Not a sample. We are doing this to your actual Friday.

Slice. Pick one section and name it by page range or heading. Not "the document". Not "the important bits", because deciding what is important is the job you are trying to check. One named slice, with an edge you could point at.

Ask. The four-part ask from session 1, plus your checklist of what must appear, plus the instruction to say "absent" rather than skip. Send it once and do not iterate yet.

Audit. Your five must-haves, written from memory before you read the reply, checked one by one. Then ask it what it left out and why. Two independent passes, and they catch different things.

Fill. Type the missing pieces in yourself. Keep a note of how many there were. That count is the honest measure of what this technology is doing for you, and it is the number you will bring to session 5.

Write your five before you ask for anything. Five things you know must be in the summary of that document. From memory, on paper or in a scratch note. Ninety seconds, no peeking at the file. This list is the only unbiased instrument you own.

Run the bad ask once, deliberately. "Summarise this document for my weekly report." Keep the answer. You need something to compare against, and it is much more convincing when it is your own document doing the disappointing.

Check your five against it. Not "does it read well". Item one: present or absent. Item two: present or absent. Most rooms find two or three missing on a genuinely long file, and the room goes quiet at that point.

Now slice. One named section, page range stated. Same four-part ask, with your five as an explicit checklist and the instruction to mark anything absent rather than skip it.

Ask the audit question. "What did you leave out of this section, and why?" You will get one of three answers: a useful list, a vague reassurance, or a claim of completeness. All three are informative, and only the first one is trustworthy.

Fill the gaps in your own words. Do not ask it to fill them. You know the exception, it does not, and this is the part of the report that was always going to be yours.

Count two numbers and keep them. How many of your five were missing from the whole-document ask, and how many from the sliced ask. That pair of numbers is worth more to you than any vendor statistic, because it was measured on the document you actually work on.

If you take one thing from tonight. Write your five before you read the answer. Everything else is technique. This one is the only thing that makes you able to see an absence at all.

Homework

Before session 3

20 min
  1. Run the omission audit on one real document. The five-item list first, then the sliced ask, then the audit question. Write down how many items were missing. One document is enough.
  2. Save the good ask as text you can paste. A note, a sticky, a snippet, wherever you will actually find it on a Friday afternoon. You will formalise this properly in session 6, but do not retype it from scratch next week.
  3. Try one deliberately cruel test. Take a document where you know a single important sentence is buried around the middle. Ask for a whole-document summary and see whether it survives. This is the experiment that converts colleagues.
  4. Bring one number to session 3. A figure from your weekly report that you cannot currently explain the derivation of. Something you copy from somewhere, or that a formula produces and you have never opened. Session 3 is about being able to defend that number when somebody asks.
  5. Keep counting your days. Same tick sheet as last week. How many days did you actually reach for it. That number is doing more work in your real result than any technique on this page.
