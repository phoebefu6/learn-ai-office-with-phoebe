LiveThe sentence tonight hangs on2 min▶

A number you cannot derive is a number you cannot defend.

Think about the last time a figure in one of your reports was challenged. Not proved wrong, just questioned. Somebody said "where does that come from?" and the honest answer was a small pause, then a scroll, then "let me check and come back to you".

That pause is the entire cost of an undefendable number, and it is expensive in a way that never shows up on a productivity dashboard. It costs you the room's confidence for the next ten minutes, and it costs you the afternoon you then spend reconstructing something you already produced once.

An assistant that hands you a total makes that pause more likely, not less, because now the derivation happened somewhere you were not watching. An assistant that hands you a formula makes it impossible, because the derivation is sitting in the cell where anybody can read it, including you, in three weeks, when you have forgotten everything.

Part 1 · covers what it genuinely does

What these assistants really do in a spreadsheet now

5 min live

Start with the capability, honestly, because this is genuinely more than it was and dismissing it makes you look uninformed. Both products now edit your sheet rather than just talking about it.

LiveThe capability list, and the gotchas that bite4 min▶

Copilot in Excel adds, renames and deletes worksheets, edits cells and ranges, applies conditional formatting and data validation, writes formulas that reach across sheets, builds charts and PivotTables, and fetches from the web with citations.

Gemini in Sheets builds tables, writes formulas, runs analysis, makes charts and pivot tables, adds dropdowns, sorts and filters, and will construct a whole sheet from a plan that you approve before it runs.

Now the three gotchas nobody mentions in the demo video:

  • Platform. Copilot's edit mode is Windows, Mac and web only. On iPad and iPhone you get chat mode only. If your habit is reviewing numbers on a tablet on the train, the thing you saw demonstrated is not the thing you have.
  • Calculation Options must be set to Automatic for editing to work. If you are one of those people who set a heavy workbook to Manual years ago and forgot, this will look like the feature is broken.
  • File format. Gemini in Sheets works best on native Sheets files, so an .xlsx has to be converted first. On the Microsoft side, a workbook saved as Strict Open XML has to be resaved as .xlsx.

Check your edition before you promise anything to your team. On Google Workspace, Business Starter has no access to the Sheets AI function, the sheet build and edit features, or match-style in Docs. Standard allows 5,000 AI function calls a month and Plus allows 25,000.

Self-studyTwo more surfaces worth knowing exist3 min▶

Agent Mode in Word, Excel and PowerPoint went generally available on 22 April 2026 and does multi-step actions in place, on desktop and mobile. A detail worth carrying because it surprises people: Agent Mode runs on OpenAI reasoning models, while Office Agent runs on Anthropic models. Same menu, different engines underneath. The Analyst agent, currently in the early-access Frontier program, "can run Python ... and you can view the code it's running in real time". Read that through tonight's lens: the valuable half of that sentence is not the Python, it is that you can watch the code.

On the Anthropic side, Claude for Excel does workbook question and answer with cell-level citations and can adjust assumptions while keeping formula relationships intact. It needs Pro, Max, Team or Enterprise, runs on web plus recent Windows and Mac builds, and is not available on the 2016 or 2019 perpetual versions, on iPad, or on Android. Data tables and macros are unsupported. Cell-level citations are the same idea again, from a third vendor: when three companies independently ship "here is where this came from", that is the market agreeing with the thesis of this session.

Part 2 · covers the vendor's own limits

The best document in this course is Microsoft telling you not to

6 min live

There is now a real worksheet function, =COPILOT(), that you type into a cell like any other. Google has its own equivalent in =AI(). Both are genuinely useful. Both come with vendor documentation that rules out the first thing almost everyone tries.

LiveWhat the =COPILOT() documentation actually says4 min▶

Straight from Microsoft's own function reference. Read these as a group, because the pattern is the lesson.

  • "COPILOT uses AI and can give incorrect responses", stated on the page for the function itself.
  • Avoid it for "Numerical calculations". Use native formulas "for any task requiring accuracy or reproducibility".
  • Avoid it for lookups. Use XLOOKUP, which is deterministic and which you already know.
  • Avoid it for legal, regulatory or compliance tasks.
  • "Formula results may change over time, even with the same arguments."

Stop on that last one, because it breaks something people assume is unbreakable. A spreadsheet's core promise is that the same inputs give the same output, forever. That is why anybody trusts a workbook at all. A cell that may quietly return something different next quarter is a different kind of object living in the same grid, and it looks identical to its neighbours.

Two more practical limits: a cap of 100 calculations per 10 minutes, after which you get a connection error, and the function cannot see the rest of your workbook. It knows the prompt and the ranges you hand it, and nothing else.

LiveGoogle's =AI() has the same shape of limits3 min▶

Different company, same physics:

  • Text only. It generates, summarizes, categorizes and scores sentiment. It is not a calculator either.
  • Only the first 350 selected cells generate per batch. Select two thousand rows and most of them quietly do nothing.
  • It cannot see the whole spreadsheet or other Drive files, exactly like the Microsoft function.
  • It cannot be nested inside IF, so the clever conditional you were about to build does not work.
  • And the one that costs people real work: it cannot be undone.
Real world

Read the two pages side by side and the message is unmistakable. Both vendors shipped a cell-level AI function, and both then published documentation saying: not for numbers, not for lookups, it cannot see your workbook, and the answer may not be stable. They are telling you what it is for. It is for the text work you used to do by hand in a column: tagging free-text feedback, drafting the comment field, standardising messy category names. That is a real job and it saves real time. It is just not the job of producing the number at the bottom of your report.

What almost everyone types first =COPILOT("what was our average monthly revenue in H1?", A2:B7)
The same job, done in the tool built for it =AVERAGE(monthly_rev) Use the AI function for the column beside it instead: =COPILOT("in three words, why did this month move?", C2:F2) And for the flag, plain logic beats a prompt every time: =IF(B2 < 0.9 * C2, "missed target", "on plan")

The second block is not more conservative, it is more correct. The average is deterministic and it will be the same number in March. The three-word explanation is language work, which is what the AI function is genuinely good at. Profit & loss lines are not the place to experiment.

Part 3 · covers the accuracy number

There is a number for this, and it needs handling carefully

4 min live

People ask "but how accurate is it, really" and deserve a real answer rather than a shrug. There is one. It comes with a caveat that matters as much as the figure.

LiveHow to quote these two figures without misleading anyone4 min▶

SpreadsheetBench reports human experts at 71.33 percent overall. Microsoft reports Agent Mode in Excel at 57.2 percent on the full task set.

Now the part that has to be said out loud every single time you use these numbers: they come from two different evaluators, and Microsoft evaluated its own product. Putting them next to each other is useful for scale and it is not a head-to-head result. If you put them on a slide as a clean comparison, you are doing the thing this course keeps warning about.

Look at the human bar for a second. Skilled people, working carefully on a benchmark of real spreadsheet tasks, came in at 71.33 percent. Spreadsheet work is hard, and part of what these figures measure is the difficulty of the task rather than the failings of the tool.

The lesson is not "it is bad". Either figure is high enough that the output usually looks right, which is precisely what makes it dangerous without a check. A tool that failed obviously would be safe. A tool that succeeds most of the time and fails silently is the one that needs a spot-check habit, and that is what the build-along installs.

Part 4 · corrects stale advice

Two things you have been told that are no longer true

2 min live

This field moves fast enough that confident advice goes off. Two corrections, both of which will make you look well informed in a meeting this month.

What gets repeatedThe current position
"Copilot in Excel needs AutoSave on and the file in OneDrive"Not true now. Microsoft's own wording: "Copilot in Excel runs in workbooks with autosave enabled or disabled." If you avoided it because of this, the reason is gone
"Copilot in Excel handles up to 2 million cells"Widely repeated and it appears on no live Microsoft page. This course does not teach a number, because a made-up threshold tells you exactly when to stop being careful

The second one is the more interesting failure. A specific figure travels much further than a vague warning, gets quoted in a deck, and eventually somebody builds a process on it. If you cannot point at the page it lives on, say "I do not have a verified number" instead. It is a better answer and it stays true.

Build-along

Rebuild one real number so the working is visible

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open the sheet behind your weekly report and find the figure you brought as homework. The one you copy from somewhere, or that a formula produces and you have never opened. We are going to make it derivable.

Look at the second metric it returns, the one labelled "after substitution". That is what showing your working means in practice: not the result, but the arithmetic the result came from, laid out so a colleague can disagree with it.

Name the range first. Select the block, give it a name a human would recognise: monthly_rev, headcount_q3, churned_accounts. Thirty seconds, and it removes most of the guessing about which rows you meant.

Ask for the formula, explicitly. "Give me the formula for the H1 average using the named range monthly_rev. Do not give me the number." That last sentence is the whole session. Say it out loud as you type it.

Read the formula before you accept it. Does it point at the range you meant? Does it include the header row by mistake? Does it quietly average six months when your report covers five? This is a ten-second read and it catches the majority of what goes wrong.

Put the working next to the number. A small note column, or a comment: "H1 average, six months, excludes the Frankfurt correction". Your future self is the main beneficiary and your future self has forgotten everything.

Spot-check two figures by hand. Not all of them, two. Pick the largest number in the report and one chosen at random, and recompute them yourself on paper or a calculator. Four minutes. Do it every week and you will find something roughly once a quarter.

Find any AI cell function hiding in your workbook, and write down what your check cost. If a colleague has put one in a numeric column, share the vendor documentation rather than an opinion: "their own page says not for numerical calculations" ends the argument politely. Then note the minutes you spent checking and anything you found, because session 5 needs both.

The cost of one wrong figure. The trap above is not a scold, it is arithmetic. A wrong number in a report that goes upward does not cost you the minutes to fix it. It costs the meeting where it is questioned, the rebuild, the corrected version you have to send round, and the small permanent tax on how carefully everyone reads your next one. Four minutes of spot-checking is cheap against that.

Homework

Before session 4

  2. Rebuild one more figure so the formula is visible. Ask for the formula, read it, put it in the cell, and write one line beside it saying what it excludes.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · According to Microsoft's own documentation, what should you avoid using =COPILOT() for?

The function page says "COPILOT uses AI and can give incorrect responses", tells you to use native formulas "for any task requiring accuracy or reproducibility", points you at XLOOKUP for lookups, rules out legal, regulatory and compliance work, and warns that "Formula results may change over time, even with the same arguments".

2 · Human experts scored 71.33 percent on SpreadsheetBench and Microsoft reports Agent Mode in Excel at 57.2 percent. What must you say when you quote them together?

Both figures are real and useful for a sense of scale, and side by side they are not a head-to-head result. The honest takeaway is not "it is bad" but "it is good enough to look right most of the time", which is exactly the condition that makes a spot-check necessary.

3 · Which of these is now out of date?

Microsoft now states that "Copilot in Excel runs in workbooks with autosave enabled or disabled". The other two are current. And the widely repeated "2 million cells" limit appears on no live Microsoft page, so this course teaches no number at all rather than a comfortable fake one.
