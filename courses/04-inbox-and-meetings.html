Inbox and meetings

Part 1 · covers the trust cost of a drafted email

A drafted email costs you trust, and there is a study

6 min live

Sessions 1 to 3 were about minutes. This one is about something you cannot get back by working late. When you let a tool write the message, the reader is not only reading the message. They are forming a view about you, and the research says that view moves.

🎫 Clear all three quiz questions to stamp your passport Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class

★ What you walk out with tonight. A line you can actually hold about which messages you write yourself, the shape of a safe integration you can point at in any vendor conversation, a notes discipline that survives what the tools drop, and the one number that ends the fewer-meetings argument for good.

LiveWhat the study actually says, and what it does not5 min▶

Cardon and Coman surveyed 1,100 working professionals in a two-by-four design: two message situations crossed with four levels of AI assistance, from none through to high. The finding in the authors' own words is that "managers who use AI for routine communication tasks put their trustworthiness at risk when using medium- to high-levels of AI assistance", with respondents beginning to question the sender's "authorship, confidence, caring, sincerity, and ability".

Honesty rule, and I want you to hold me to it. You will see this study quoted with crisp percentages attached. Those percentages come from a university press release, not from the article body. So this course teaches the direction and the design: 1,100 professionals, two by four, medium and high assistance puts trustworthiness at risk. If someone quotes you a decimal from this study, ask them which page it is on.

Notice what the study does not say. It does not say never use it. Low assistance did not carry the same penalty. The damage sits at the point where the reader can no longer tell whether the words are yours.

Self-studyThe practical line, and the three messages you always write yourself4 min▶

The line that survives contact with a real inbox is not a policy, it is a habit:

  • AI for structure and the first pass. Turning six scattered facts into a clean five-point update is exactly the reachable band from session 1. Let it do that.
  • Your own words for anything relational. The sentence that acknowledges, reassures, thanks or apologises is the sentence the reader weighs you on. Type it.
  • Never for these three. A message carrying an apology. A message carrying a decision about a person. A message carrying thanks. Those three are the whole of the trust risk in one line, and they are cheap to write because they are short.

There is a practical tell for whether you have crossed the line. If you would be embarrassed for the recipient to know how the message was produced, it was produced wrong. That is not a moral test, it is a prediction about what happens if they find out.

"Write an email to Sam apologising for missing the deadline and explaining the new date."

Back comes a fluent, balanced, slightly warm paragraph that regrets the inconvenience and values the partnership. It is well written. It is also unmistakably not you, and Sam has read forty of these this month. What Sam takes from it is that the apology was not worth your time.

"Give me a three-part structure for a slipped-deadline note: what happened, the new date, what I am doing differently. Bullets only, no prose, no apology line."

You get the skeleton in ten seconds. Then you type the first sentence yourself: "Sam, I missed this and I should have told you on Tuesday rather than today." Twenty words, entirely yours, and they are the only twenty Sam will remember.

Run that seed through and look at the words-per-sentence number. Fluent, long, and it says nothing a person could be held to. That is the texture readers are learning to spot, and it is why the relational sentence has to be short and yours.

Part 2 · covers what a safe integration looks like

The design pattern worth copying: nothing sends itself

4 min live

There is one product decision in this whole space that I would put on a wall, and it is not a feature. It is a permission that was deliberately not requested.

LiveThe permission that was not requested4 min▶

Anthropic's Claude for Outlook add-in drafts replies and invites. Its documentation states the property plainly: "Claude never sends mail or invites on its own. The add-in does not request the Mail.Send permission."

Read that twice, because it is a stronger statement than a setting. A toggle that says "ask before sending" can be turned off by you, by an admin, or by a future release. A permission that was never requested cannot be quietly enabled later without a visible consent prompt. The safety is structural, not configured.

Real world

Every draft lands unsent and a human clicks send. That is the whole pattern, and it is the question to ask about any assistant anyone tries to put into your inbox: what permissions does it hold, and can it act without me? If the answer involves a checkbox rather than an absence, you have a different risk profile.

The caveat, said out loud: the Claude for Outlook add-in is beta. I am teaching the shape, not recommending a rollout. The shape outlives the product.

Self-studyWhere Copilot in Outlook can and cannot reach3 min▶

Same section, different vendor, and this one bites people on day one. Copilot in Outlook drafts, summarizes a thread, coaches on clarity, sentiment and tone, and prioritizes the inbox. What it does not do:

  • Primary Exchange Online mailboxes only. Not archive mailboxes, not group mailboxes, not shared mailboxes, not delegate mailboxes.
  • It cannot touch S/MIME or Double Key Encryption mail.

If your team runs a shared support mailbox, which is exactly the inbox where triage help would be worth the most, that is the one it cannot see. Worth knowing before you build a workflow around it.

Structure only, nothing relationalTurn these six notes into a three-point update for the ops lead. Bullets only. No greeting, no sign-off, no apology, no thanks. Keep every figure exactly as written. If something is missing, say so. I will write the opening and closing lines myself.
Part 3 · covers meeting notes and what they leave out

Meeting notes, and the things they quietly drop

6 min live

Automatic meeting notes are the most genuinely useful feature in either product. They are also the one where session 2's lesson bites hardest, because the failure is omission and omission does not look wrong.

LiveCopilot in Teams: five documented limits4 min▶
  • Chat summary caps at 30 days, and one thread only. The long-running channel where the decision actually happened is out of reach.
  • It "cannot summarize images, Loop components, or files shared in the chat". Those are Microsoft's own words, and in most teams that is where half the substance lives.
  • Answers degrade over roughly two-hour meetings. Your longest, most consequential session is the one it handles worst.
  • Eight supported languages. Check yours before you build a habit on it.
  • Everything dies if the transcript is deleted. Retention policy is a feature dependency, which almost nobody realises until the recap is gone.

Notice that four of those five are omissions rather than errors. The recap will be fluent and confident about the ninety minutes it did see, and completely silent about the Loop component that held the actual decision.

Self-studyGoogle Meet take notes for me: same shape, different edges3 min▶

Meet's "Take notes for me" writes a Doc with Summary, Decisions and Next Steps into the organizer's Drive. Its documented edges:

  • Eight languages, and one at a time. Google's own line: "This feature supports one language at a time. Multiple languages spoken in the same meeting aren't currently supported." If your team code-switches mid-call, and plenty do, that is a real gap.
  • 15 minutes to 8 hours. Shorter than 15 and you get nothing.
  • Not retroactive. Notes cover from the moment someone starts them. The first ten minutes, where the framing usually happens, are gone.
  • Google's own disclaimer: summaries "can be incomplete, inaccurate, or not generated".

That last line is unusually honest for a product page and it is the one to quote when someone proposes replacing a human note-taker on a governance call.

Decide what matters. Before the call, write down the three things that must appear in the notes. Not ten. Three. A decision, an owner, a date is a good default. This takes ninety seconds and it is the entire trick.

Record. Start the notes at the very top of the call, because they are not retroactive. If someone joins late and re-frames the meeting, that re-frame is only captured if the notes were already running.

Check the three. Open the generated notes and look for your three items specifically. You are not reading for quality, you are checking a list. This is a thirty-second job and it is the only step that catches an omission.

Fill the gap. Anything on your list that is not in the notes, you add by hand and mark as added. Now the document is trustworthy, and everyone who reads it later can see which parts a person vouched for.

Part 4 · covers the finding nobody quotes

Meetings did not go down

3 min live

This is the short part and it is the one you will still remember in a year. Every deck that has ever been shown to you about this technology implies you will end up in fewer meetings. The best available measurement says otherwise, and it was run by the vendor.

LiveSay it plainly3 min▶

Microsoft ran a randomized controlled trial across 56 firms and over 6,000 workers for six months, measured on objective telemetry rather than self-report. Two results matter here:

  • Email reading fell 12.3 minutes a week. Real, measured, and it is the honest win of this session. Your inbox does get lighter.
  • Total meeting duration came out at plus 2.38 minutes a week, and was not statistically significant. Not a small decrease. Not a decrease at all.

A separate Microsoft telemetry study across 47 organizations found 10 where meetings fell and 14 where meetings rose. More organizations went up than down.

Real world

The vendor funded the research, ran it on its own product, and published the null. That is worth respecting, and it is also why the number is so useful to you: it is the least likely finding to be flattering, and it survived anyway. Anyone promising you fewer meetings is selling.

Which is why this course never counts meeting time as a saving. The simulator has a lever for it that moves the number by exactly zero, and it exists to be switched on and disappoint you.

Build-along

A triage pass on your real inbox, then one real meeting

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Two exercises on live material. The inbox one takes twelve minutes and you do it now. The meeting one takes ninety seconds of preparation and you do it on the next call in your calendar.

Open your inbox and take the last twenty messages. Not a sample folder. The real thing, with the real backlog and the one you have been avoiding since Tuesday.

Sort them into three piles by hand. Pile one: needs no reply. Pile two: needs a factual reply, no relationship at stake. Pile three: relational, meaning it carries an apology, thanks, or a decision about a person. Most people find pile three is three or four messages out of twenty.

Pile one gets summarised, never drafted. Ask for a five-line digest of what happened in the thread. You are buying reading time, which is the saving the randomized trial actually measured.

Pile two gets a structured first pass. Attach the thread, name the reader, give the shape, add the honesty clause from session 1. Then edit it, because it will get a fact slightly wrong.

Pile three you write yourself, and time it. Three messages, all short, probably under nine minutes total. That number is the real price of the trust rule, and it is smaller than everyone assumes before they measure it.

Now pick one real meeting from your calendar this week. Before it starts, write down the three things that must appear in the notes. Put them somewhere you will see afterwards.

After the meeting, check the generated notes against your three. Do not read the notes for quality. Check the list. Count how many of your three are missing, and keep that number, because it is the single most useful piece of evidence you will collect all course.

Fill the gaps by hand and mark them as added. Then send the notes with a line saying which parts you added. That line is what makes the document usable by anyone who was not there.

If you take one thing from tonight. Three messages a week are relational and you write those yourself. Everything else in the inbox is fair game. That rule takes under ten minutes a week to honour and it is the cheapest insurance in this course.

Homework

Before session 5

25 min
  1. Run the triage on a full week. Count how many messages land in pile three. Write the number down. Almost everyone overestimates it before counting and underestimates its weight after.
  2. Do the three-things exercise on two more meetings. Keep the miss count for each. Two data points is enough to know whether your notes tool is dropping things systematically or occasionally.
  3. Check the permission question on whatever is in your inbox. Whatever assistant your organisation has enabled, find out whether it can send on your behalf. If it can, find out how that is controlled. You will need this answer in session 6.
  4. Find one meeting input your report actually needs. A decision, a number, a commitment. Session 5 assembles the report and this is the input that comes from here.
  5. Bring your report's current state. Numbers from session 3, written frame from session 2, and now one meeting input. Session 5 puts them together end to end.
