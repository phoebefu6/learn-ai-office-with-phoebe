The rung that has been locked since session 1

Open the simulator from session 1 and switch every lever on. Until tonight it stops at 122 minutes, 34 percent faster on the task, and 4.8 percent of your week. There is one more rung and it is not a cleverer prompt. It is the same prompt, saved, and reached for on more days.

★ The thesis, said plainly. Every measured-workweek study in this course reports a few percent rather than forty. The reason is not that the tool is worse in the wild. LiveWhere the last six points actually come from2 min▶

On the task, the ladder you have climbed across five sessions reads: 170 minutes at 8 percent for a bare ask, 150 at 19 percent once the data is attached and the reader named, 137 at 26 percent asking for a slice, 128 at 31 percent making it show its working, 122 at 34 percent once you check the figures. Every one of those was a change to how you ask.

The last rung, 111 minutes and 40 percent, is not a change to how you ask. It is not asking again at all. You wrote the ask once in session 1 and refined it over four sessions. Retyping it every Friday is pure overhead you are paying for no reason.

On the week the same thing shows up differently, and more honestly: 4.8 percent becomes 5.7 percent, which is 136 minutes, which sits just above the 5.4 percent that Bick, Blandin and Deming measured among actual users. You do not get there with a better sentence. You get there with a Friday reminder.

Part 1 · covers the saved prompt

Rewriting the ask every week is most of the overhead

5 min live

Your good ask is roughly forty to sixty words and it took you five sessions to arrive at. Almost all of it is identical week to week. Only one part actually changes, and once you can see which part, the whole thing becomes a template you fill rather than a paragraph you compose.

You open a blank box and start typing. You half remember the wording that worked. You forget the honesty clause because it felt like padding. You get a confident draft with one silently missing exception, and you spend the afternoon finding it. Next week you start from blank again.

You paste a saved template and replace two square brackets. Every hard-won instruction is still there because you never had to remember it. The output is the one you already know how to check. Elapsed time before you hit send: about forty seconds.

LiveAnatomy of a reusable ask, with the variable parts marked4 min▶

Take the four-part ask from session 1 and put square brackets around anything that changes. The brackets are not magic syntax, they are a visual cue to you: these are the only bits you touch.

Saved weekly report ask

Here is [this week's ops sheet] and [last week's report]. Write the weekly ops summary for [the ops lead] in five bullets, then a table with the three headline figures and the percentage change on each. Keep every figure exactly as it appears in the sheet. Flag anything that moved more than 10 percent. Show me which cell or row each figure came from. If a number is missing, say so and do not estimate. Do not smooth over an exception, name it.

Three things make this survive week to week. Nothing dated is baked in - no "for August", no "since the reorg", those go in the brackets or they rot. The honesty clause lives inside the template, so you cannot skip it on a busy Friday, which is exactly the Friday you would have skipped it. And it names the checkable thing: "show me which cell each figure came from" is what makes your session 5 check take two minutes instead of twenty.

Version it like a document, not like a note. When you change the wording, keep the old one for a week. If the output gets worse you want to be able to go back, and you will not remember what you changed.

Self-studyWhere to keep it so you actually find it again3 min▶

A saved prompt in a place you have to go looking for is not saved, it is lost with extra steps. Ranked by how likely you are to still be using it in a month:

  • Pinned in the place you do the work. A note at the top of the report file itself, or the first row of a hidden tab in the workbook. Zero navigation.
  • A text expander or clipboard snippet. Type a short trigger, the whole ask appears. This is the lowest-friction option that exists.
  • Microsoft's Copilot Prompt Gallery. It exists, it is a starting point rather than a personal store, and it is worth a browse before you write yours from scratch.
  • Claude's Office add-ins have a persistent Instructions field per app. Anything you put there applies every time you use that add-in, which makes it the natural home for the fixed three parts.

Whichever you pick, the test is the same: can you get from "it is Friday" to "the ask is in the box" without opening a search bar?

Part 2 · covers habit, and what the time is really worth

Habit over heroics, and the counterweight nobody puts next to it

6 min live

Here is the finding this whole course has been circling. In week mode the simulator has a dial marked days per week, and dragging it is more powerful than every prompt technique in these six sessions put together. Nothing about the tool changes as you drag. Only how often you reached for it.

LiveWhy the measured studies land so low, in one sentence3 min▶

Bick, Blandin and Deming measured time savings equivalent to 5.4 percent of hours among users and 1.4 percent across all workers, which they put at 2.2 hours a week for users. Put that against the simulator ladder above and the picture resolves instantly: 5.4 percent is roughly the five-days-a-week row. Most people are living somewhere around rows one and two.

Microsoft's own randomized trial across 56 firms and over 6,000 workers reported that "nearly 40% of workers who were given access used it regularly". The other sixty percent are not badly prompting. They are not prompting.

Real world

The distance between the headline number and your lived experience is almost never a skill gap. It is a calendar gap. A person on the two-days row who moves to four days gains 55 minutes a week without learning a single new technique.

LiveThe honest counterweight, and what it means for a real person4 min▶

Humlum and Vestergaard followed 25,000 workers across 7,000 workplaces in Denmark. Their current revision estimates "precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours ... ruling out effects larger than 2%" two years after the launch of ChatGPT. And the mechanism, from the same work: 85 percent of users report reallocating time savings from AI chatbots to other job tasks, while fewer than 10 percent take extra breaks.

Read that carefully, because it is not a reason to stop. It is a reason to be specific about what you are buying. The minutes are real - nobody in that data disputes the time was saved. The minutes do not stay yours - absent a decision, the work expands to fill them and your recorded hours look identical. So decide in advance what the 136 minutes are for: leaving on time on Fridays, the analysis you never get to, or actually thinking about what the report should say. If you do not name it, the answer defaults to more of what you were already doing.

Say the honest version to your manager too: "this makes Friday shorter, it does not make me 40 percent more productive". You will be the only person in the room who has read the workweek numbers, and it is a much more defensible claim than the one on the slide.

Part 3 · covers oversharing

The risk nobody expects: your permissions were always like this

5 min live

Microsoft's own documentation says it in five words: "generative AI amplifies the problem of oversharing data", because content may be "obsolete, over-permissioned, or lack governance controls". That sentence has been misread as "the AI leaks things". It does not. What it does is much stranger and much more common.

LiveThe rule underneath, and the exact framing to use4 min▶

Both vendors state the permission rule clearly. Copilot "only surfaces organizational data to which individual users have at least view permissions". Google's version of the same sentence: "If you don't have permission to see a file, the AI can't see it or use it either."

So here is the framing, and teach it exactly this way, because it is the version that is both true and useful:

★ Neither product breaks permissions. Both make a decade of sloppy permissions instantly searchable in plain language.

That folder someone shared with "everyone in the company" in 2019 so one contractor could see one file was always readable by you. You just never would have found it. Now you can ask a question in a sentence and it will answer from it, correctly and within policy, and the result will still make everybody uncomfortable. The failure is a governance failure that predates the assistant by years. The assistant is the flashlight, not the mess.

Self-studyThe admin levers, which are IT's job and not yours3 min▶

Name these so you can raise them with the right people. Do not attempt to configure them yourself, and be suspicious of any course that tells an end user to.

If something surfaces that clearly should not be readable by you, you have just done your IT team a genuine favour. Send them the finding, not a screenshot of the contents.

Part 4 · covers prompt injection

Instructions can arrive inside an ordinary office file

4 min live

This one sounds like a security-conference problem until you notice the delivery mechanism. It is not a shady link. It is a spreadsheet a vendor emailed you, and it looks exactly like every other spreadsheet a vendor has ever emailed you.

LiveWhat the vendors actually say, in their own words3 min▶
  • Anthropic, on Word. Injections hide "in document content such as text, comments, tracked changes, headers, and footers". Every one of those is a place you do not look and the add-in does.
  • Anthropic, on Excel. "Only use Claude for Excel with trusted spreadsheets. Files from external sources can contain hidden instructions that manipulate the add-in into extracting data, modifying records, or performing destructive actions."
  • Microsoft. There are XPIA classifiers in place, with the caveat stated plainly: "These classifiers may not be available in all Microsoft Copilot scenarios." A defence that may or may not be present is not a defence you can plan around.

The practical rule, and it is short. A spreadsheet from a vendor is an external file. So is the deck from the agency, the tracker from the client, and the template someone downloaded. Open it, look at the comments and the tracked changes, and only then let an add-in act on it. If it is doing anything destructive or extractive on your behalf, that is the moment to be paying attention.

Part 5 · covers the limits

What never to automate, and why that is a relief

3 min live

Six sessions of building a machine, and the last piece is a list of what you deliberately keep out of it. This list is not a warning. It is the part of your job that was never the problem, and drawing a line around it is what makes the rest safe to speed up.

Self-studyThe four, in full, and the vendor version of the same list3 min▶
  • Anything relational. The apology, the decision about a person, the thank you, the hard conversation. Session 4 has the study: managers using medium to high levels of AI assistance for routine communication put their trustworthiness at risk, with people questioning authorship, sincerity and caring. The cost is not accuracy, it is you.
  • Anything audit critical without verification. Not "never automate the numbers" - session 3 was entirely about automating the numbers. The rule is that no figure leaves without a human having opened the source. If you will not do the check, do not generate the figure.
  • Anything with legal or compliance weight. Microsoft's own documentation for the =COPILOT() function explicitly lists legal, regulatory and compliance uses among the things it is not for, and notes that "formula results may change over time, even with the same arguments". A number that can quietly change is not an auditable number.
  • The decision about what the report should say. This is the one people give away without noticing. Drafting is a service. Deciding what matters this week is why anyone reads your report rather than the raw sheet.

The vendors say a softer version of the same thing in their own not-recommended lists. When a product's own documentation tells you what it is not for, that is the single most reliable sentence on the page, because nobody writes it for marketing reasons.

Build-along

Save the ask, set the trigger, then check what you can see

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Three things tonight, and none of them are clever. Write the saved prompt for your report and put it somewhere you will find it. Set the trigger that makes Friday happen without you deciding. Then spend ninety seconds finding out what your permissions actually expose.

Saved ask. The four parts written once, with square brackets around the only thing that changes. Stored where you can reach it without searching.

Trigger. A calendar block or a recurring task on the day it is due. Without this you are relying on remembering, and the days dial is the whole ballgame.

Run. Paste, swap the brackets, send. Forty seconds. This is the step everyone thinks is the whole thing, and it is the cheapest one.

Check. The session 5 pass: every figure against its source, and one deliberate look for what is missing rather than what is wrong.

Improve the ask. Whatever you had to fix this week, add one sentence to the template so you never fix it again. The loop gets cheaper every time round.

Around eighty words is normal and healthy for a saved prompt, because you only pay for those words once. The four-word ask from session 1 was not efficient, it was under-specified. The difference now is that length costs you nothing at all.

Write the template. Take the ask you have been refining since session 1 and put square brackets around every part that changes week to week. If more than one thing is in brackets, look again, it is usually just the material.

Store it somewhere with zero navigation. Pinned in the report file, in a clipboard snippet, or in the persistent Instructions field of the add-in you use. Say the location out loud to yourself, because "I will remember" is how the last four templates died.

Set the trigger now, in this session. A recurring calendar block on the day the report is due, or a repeating task. Give it the name of the report, not "AI time". You are scheduling the job, not the tool.

Decide what the saved minutes are for. Write it in the calendar note. Eighty five percent of users put the time into other job tasks by default. That is fine if you chose it and quietly corrosive if you did not.

Now the permissions check. Search your own organizational content by plain-English description rather than by filename. "Compensation review", "redundancy plan", "the pricing model for the renewal". Anything a person would say out loud.

Look at what came back, then stop. If something surfaced that you were not aware you could read, do not open it further and do not screenshot the contents. Note the location and send it to whoever owns SharePoint or Drive. You have found a permissions problem that existed long before this tool did. Then do the provenance sweep: any file feeding your report that arrived from outside your team gets read by you, comments and tracked changes included, before an add-in acts on it.

If you take one thing from tonight. The calendar block is worth more than the template. A perfect saved prompt you reach for once a week is 27 minutes. A merely decent one you reach for every day is 136.

Homework

The last one, and it takes four weeks

4 weeks · 5 min a week
  1. Run it for four weeks and tick the days you actually used it. Not the days you meant to. A row of four numbers, one per week. That tick count is the honest version of every productivity claim you have ever been shown, including the ones on this page.
  2. Improve the template once a week. One sentence, from whatever you had to fix. Four weeks is four improvements and the ask stops needing them.
  3. Go back to session 1's simulator with a full passport. The top rung is unlocked now. Switch everything on in task mode, see 111 minutes and 40 percent, then flip to week mode and put your real tick count in the days dial. That second number is yours, not a study's.
  4. Check the trap once. Switch on "skip the check and send it out" and watch 5.7 percent fall to 4.4. Then switch on "expect it to give you fewer meetings" and watch nothing happen at all, one last time.
  5. Say the honest number to one other person. Whoever asked you what this was worth. Two numbers, not one: what it does to the task, and what it does to the week.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and the passport is full.

1 · In week mode, what moves the number from 27 minutes to 136?

1 day is 27 minutes and 1.1 percent of working hours. 5 days is 136 minutes and 5.7 percent. Same levers, same tool, same prompt. Only the days dial moved, which is why habit is the mechanism rather than a tip.

2 · What is the correct thing to say about oversharing?

Copilot "only surfaces organizational data to which individual users have at least view permissions", and Google's version is that if you cannot see a file, the AI cannot either. Microsoft still warns that generative AI "amplifies the problem of oversharing data", because the content may be over-permissioned already. The boundary did not move. Findability did.

3 · Humlum and Vestergaard found 85 percent of users reallocate saved time to other job tasks. What should you do with that?

The minutes are real. Their current revision also estimates precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects larger than 2 percent, and fewer than 10 percent of users take extra breaks. Time returns as more work unless you deliberately decide otherwise. That is a choice, not an outcome.
